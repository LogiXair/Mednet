§03

First principles

01

Round-trip, not one-way

Every mission is outbound + inbound on the same flight. A drone that only delivers is doing half the job the hub needs.

02

Unprepared-site ops

Launch and recovery from a PHC courtyard or a highway marker, not a paved pad. That's the actual infra gap in India.

03

Docking autonomy

Aircraft live at unmanned hub nodes and launch on trigger. Per-flight labour cost has to trend toward zero.

04

Built to spec, not inherited

A purpose-built airframe sized to this market's numbers (20km, 120km/h, 3kg), not a scaled variant of an unrelated platform.

05

Certify to the corridor

DGCA's BVLOS approvals move fastest inside designated medical corridors. Win corridor by corridor, not nationally on day one.

§05

How a request actually moves

Modeled on the confirmation flow India's home-diagnostics networks already run (Redcliffe Labs, Tata 1mg): WhatsApp confirmation isn't a new behaviour to teach a nurse or a patient. It's already the default.

1

Request

Ward nurse or lab tech sends a WhatsApp/app request: sample type, pickup point, urgency tag.

2

Dispatch

Hub software matches the nearest aircraft and files a flight plan inside the pre-cleared corridor.

3

Launch

Aircraft lifts off unattended from the dock, no ground crew required.

4

In-flight

Live tracking link pushed to the requesting ward; bay logs temperature and vibration throughout.

5

Land & handoff

Lands at the destination dock; staff scan a chain-of-custody tag; aircraft recharges for relaunch.

6

Confirm

WhatsApp confirmation to both ends, with timestamp and flight log, closes the loop.

Same flow for both service lines. First Aid as a Service compresses steps 1-3 into an automatic trigger from a control-room call instead of a typed request, everything after stays identical.

§06

Beyond the two flagship lines

Once the hub network exists for diagnostic delivery and trauma response, the same docks and airframes extend to these largely without new hardware. This is the CFO argument: one capex line, six use cases inside a network the chain already owns.

Proven in India

Frozen-section relay

Mid-surgery tissue transport between a chain's peripheral and tertiary campuses, deciding resection margins in real time instead of waiting on a courier.

Net-new

Blood bank load-balancing

Shifting O-negative or plasma stock between a chain's own blood banks during a local shortage, instead of an emergency courier run.

Proven globally

Organ & tissue transport

Cornea, kidney, and graft transport between a chain's transplant centres, cutting cold-ischemia time on the clock that matters most.

Net-new

Chemo & specialty drug relay

Temperature-sensitive oncology drugs moved same-day between campuses instead of next-day courier scheduling.

Net-new, consumer-facing

Discharge pharmacy refill

Subscription medicines for chronic-care patients routed to the nearest hub node, skipping a pharmacy queue entirely.

Net-new

Disaster / mass-casualty surge

The same docked airframes flex from routine diagnostic runs to bulk trauma-kit airdrop during a mass-casualty event.

Sources: frozen-section relay per Kasturba Hospital/ICMR, 2024 (§02); organ and tissue transport per UMMC, 2019 and Toronto General, 2024 (§02). Remaining lines are proposed extensions of the same hub network, not yet piloted anywhere.

§07

Platform

One airframe, sized for both missions

A VTOL platform designed from a blank sheet against this market's numbers rather than adapted from an unrelated programme. Same aircraft, same payload bay, for the scheduled diagnostic run and the on-demand trauma dispatch; the two missions share a hub network, not just a company name.

Dual-mission by design: cold-chain-rated bay for the diagnostic leg, quick-swap trauma-kit mount for the response leg. Electric VTOL for unprepared-site launch and recovery, with a fast dock-to-dock turnaround so one airframe can cover both duty cycles in a day.
